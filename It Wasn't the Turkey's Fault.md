# It Wasn't the Turkey's Fault

The worst thing about the holiday was not the turkey. The worst thing about the holiday was that certain individuals simply could not resist pushing buttons. As the oldest of four, I somehow got designated the third parent and official keeper for my brothers and sister. The twins, River and Clay, are five years younger than me. That was bad enough, trying to keep up with them, but Mom and Pop decided they needed a girl five years later. I guess they thought the spacing was right, but from my perspective it only meant trouble.

Lea, for the most part, looked harmless enough. Big blue eyes. Strawberry blonde hair with just enough curl to give the impression more was there than there was. And a smile from early on that reached all the way to her eyes and just melted your heart. She weaponized the smile a lot to get out of trouble. Most everyone looked at her and saw a sweet little girl. River and Clay looked at her and saw a target because they were no longer the babies.

I wasn’t fooled. I knew she was going to have a temper, but they kept saying, “Jack, it will be fine. They all have tempers at that age. She’ll learn to control it.” At eight months old, she was having a tantrum because Clay and River were playing keep-away with her pacifier. At eighteen months old, it was pelting Clay and River with her building blocks because they kept knocking over her tower. At two and a half it was her dumping their cereal bowls on their heads because they took her spot in front of the TV and changed the channel from her favorite cartoon. Still, they said, “Jack, it’s just being a toddler. She will learn.”

Trouble was she wasn’t the one learning to control her temper. River and Clay were learning which buttons activated that trigger. Tug a ponytail here. Poke her there. Hide her teddy bear. Pointing this out was also shrugged off by the adults as, ‘that’s what brother’s do.’ I will admit I did my fair share of teasing her as I did to the twins, but never to the point where she would want retribution. At least I don’t think I did. The other part of it was that somehow I was responsible for teaching her to control her temper and not let the twins push her to that point. When did I get drafted as an adult? I still haven’t figured out.

Then she turned five and it went from bad to worse. She was now in school. Which I will admit did help take the pressure off of me a bit, but that meant the pressure between the three built up to be released in the evenings, weekends, and holidays. In our family, five is when you get your first chore on the ranch. Usually, it’s feeding the chickens and collecting the eggs, helping bottle feed the calves and foals, or something simple enough but helpful for a five year old. Lea took over the chicken duties from the twins. She was good and fast, but the chickens didn’t like her much. Then I discovered why.

It was spring break and we were visiting Papaw and Gran-ma-ma at the estate and Clay and River were already inside washing up but Lea was not answering the dinner bell, so to speak. I started looking for her in the gardens and hoping to find her there and not somewhere in the forest nearby. She wasn’t there, nor the barn, or any of her usual haunts on the estate. I headed towards the river knowing she liked to sit there and read under the willow tree. I came around the bend and sure enough she was there. Only she wasn’t alone. She was leaning against a cougar, reading a story from her book. The cougar was looking at the book like she understood the words and pictures, an almost-smile on her face. Lea had been blessed by her animal totem.

I got her attention and motioned it was time to eat. She told the cougar, “Finish it tomorrow.” Then she hugged her cougar and skipped off to join me. When we arrived at the estate and I was asked where I found her, I told them. This changed everything from a simple dinner to a celebration. Now we had all been blessed by our animals. Only, I think, now looking back on it, maybe it wasn’t such a good blessing after all. I had my raven of course, River had his otter, and Clay had his boar, but this felt different. A cougar fit Lea, only too well.

It didn’t change anything where River and Clay were concerned. It was just a new button to push. First it was little things like River flicking water at Lea and her hissing at him. Clay trying to take something away from Lea and she’d start making low growling noises. I asked Mama and Gran-ma-ma, and they said, “Well, she’s a cougar. What do you expect?” They were still unconcerned until she let out a cougar scream after the twins had unraveled all of the potholders she had been making with her loom kit she had gotten for her birthday. The truly devilish part was they cut the cotton loops, so they would no longer fit onto the loom. That was the day the inner cougar came out, so to speak.

For the record, I did try to warn them all, including trying to find something better for Clay and River to spend their time on. After the cougar scream brought us all running, we stood there watching Lea stand in the middle of the destruction, crying and upset. Then River and Clay started laughing at her. That was their fatal error, because now she was mad. She turned red at first and then started hissing and spitting like any angry cat, but the thing I had been most afraid of happened. She went from angry young child to angry young cougar cub in the blink of an eye. Then she launched an attack after the twins and somehow I was now both responsible for creating the mess, catching Lea before she murdered somebody, and cleaning it all up.

No one ever listened to me. The warning signs were there, I documented them sometimes with bandages and monkey’s blood. It shouldn’t have been a surprise to anyone, me least of all, that Thanksgiving dinner turned out the way it did. I should have seen the signs when, first thing that morning, Clay and River stole her bacon and left her with only one dollar-sized pancake for breakfast. I offered to make her some toast, but Mama and Gran-ma-ma shooed us out of the kitchen because they had to get the dinner started.

All throughout the morning, all I heard from Lea was, “I’m hungry.” All I could do was tell her, “I know.” River offered to take her fishing at the pond, but the last time they did that Lea somehow wound up an almost-drowned, muddy, furry mess. Mama told me to keep them clean and out of trouble. Then Clay tried to negotiate with Lea. He was responsible for keeping the fire going for the smokehouse where Pop was finishing the last of the meats smoking and making jerky. He told her he would keep watch while she sneaked in to get some jerky. Only, he went and told Pop and Uncle Walt what she was doing.

Then the other families started to arrive. Mama brought out the snacking trays full of deviled eggs, stuffed celery sticks, olives, and pickles. Lea was too short to reach them on the side table and both River and Clay would get one and offer it to her, only to pop it in their mouth at the last second. By the time I became aware of what was going on, Clay had popped the last pickle into his mouth. I kept asking, “How much longer?” And they kept answering, “Soon,” only soon was started four hours ago.

Finally, after getting the rickety card table set up for the four of us kids, I thought I could finally get some peace. I was so very wrong. I got our drinks poured and we sat down waiting for the adults to get seated as well. The table was finally set with the side dishes and drinks when Mama brought out the turkey and placed it in front of Pop. He held his hands out. I copied Pop at the kid’s table knowing that prayer and thanks had to come before we could finally eat. We bowed our heads, closed our eyes, and Pop began when I heard a growling next to me. I opened one eye. It wasn’t Lea making the sound, but it was her stomach. She shifted in her seat, trying to ignore it and be good.

Another growl came from her stomach, this time longer and louder. I’m thinking, *Hurry up Pop,* but he just kept going.

Lea was wiggling in her seat, her eyes closed as tight as she could make them. *C’mon, Pop say ‘Amen’ already*, I thought to myself. Too late. Lea’s eyes popped open and she locked on to the steaming bird at the adult table.

“Lea. No.” I uttered, but she did not respond.

A little louder, “Lea. No.” She didn’t hear me.

Finally, Pop said ‘Amen’ just as Lea turned into what would normally be an adorable spotted, fluffy fuzzball with big blue eyes. But in this case, this was an apex predator stalking prey. She leapt from her chair onto our table and it started to shake, causing the drinks to spill and the plates to rattle against the silverware. She crouched low and stalked slowly across the table to the edge. River and Clay looked on in surprise and amusement. I looked on in horror. She gave a small butt wiggle and launched to the adult’s table. The poor card table collapsed a leg under the force and there went our drinks, plates, and silverware to the floor.

Everyone turned to look at the commotion, failing to see Lea was preparing to land on the table. She hit the edge at just the right angle that the polish job Mama had River do earlier acted more like a hockey rink than glossy wooden table. Lea slid and skidded down the table, knocking over the serving dishes, glasses, plates, and other objects until she stopped just short of the turkey. She crouched low again and with a last butt-wiggle attacked the turkey. She started gnawing and chomping at the bird until finally she got her prize, the turkey leg. Looking around wild-eyed and daring anyone to stop her, she bolted off the table and down the hall. Everyone except River and Clay was staring at the mangled bird, wondering what just happened, while they laughed hysterically.

Mama looked at me and I was like, “Yeah, yeah, I know. Find Lea.” Pop, Papaw, and Uncle Walt grabbed the oven mitts from the kitchen and handed me a pair. We started searching the house but couldn’t find her. Standing in the living room, wondering if she might have gotten out, Mama and Gran-ma-ma joined us. As we discussed where to look next, Gran-ma-ma motioned for us to be quiet.

She looked around and then zeroed in on the couch. She motioned for Papaw and Uncle Walt to move the couch out of the way. There behind the couch was Lea. Turkey leg as big as she was between her paws. The leg was half eaten and she was low-growling and hissing at us. She guarded it like it was the last meal on earth.

Jack looks at Aaron, Kevin, Davey, and Frank for a reaction, but they are just wide-eyed. Then the laughter finally breaks out. Kevin recovers first. “I guess that teaches us to ask how it was growing up with Lea.”

Jack just smirks at them. “I told you already, I’m an exhausted older brother who has spent fifty-plus years monitoring a walking natural disaster I love more than life itself.”
