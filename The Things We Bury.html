The Things We Bury

50 Prompts, 1 Story

“It was the last day in her apartment,” she heard the landlord say to a new couple looking at her curiously as she locked the door. With the help of her dad, brother, and sister and her sister’s husband, she was moving into her grandmother’s house today. Everything from the apartment was packed and already at her grandmother’s. The last thing she packed was her office space.

She was mentally checking off the list in her head and was almost done. Then the trunk lid slammed shut and her dad looked at her and smiled. “Stop going by memory. Let’s make one final sweep.”

They went up to her apartment, but when they got to the door it was ajar. She looked over at her dad in surprise. “The door was locked when I left, but not when I returned.”

They swung the door open and looked around. It was empty except a file box on the desk. Her dad asked, “How did we miss that?”

She replied, “I don’t think we did.”

They walked over and looked at it carefully. The address was from her ex. A note on top said, “This box came from the lawyer’s for your grandmother’s estate. I put in some things from there I took by mistake. Sorry, I missed you.”

They grabbed the box, took one last look around and made sure the place was empty. As one does in these cases, she deposited her keys with the landlord. She left for her grandmother’s house with her dad and things to begin the unpacking in a place she hardly knew, but remembered fondly. The last thing that went through her head was what else was in that box?

The day started like any other Wednesday, and she carefully gathered the papers and placed them on the desk before realizing she didn’t remember buying the leather folder among them. Inside were photographs of her grandmother’s house, each showing one extra person in the background. That was strange enough, but stranger still was the note tucked inside: The things we bury don’t always stay quiet.

A piece of what looked to be a shower curtain divided the next photographs. She didn’t expect to find a body when she moved the shower curtain, but the next photograph stopped her cold. A man lay half-submerged in a claw-foot bathtub, one pale arm hanging over the side. The photograph after that showed the same scene from another angle. The third included evidence markers. By the fourth, there was no mistaking it. They were crime scene photographs. Worse still, the dead man was the extra person from every other photograph.

It took a second for her to make out the words written in blue ink along the bottom edge of the photograph. At first she thought they were part of the evidence marker, but they had been added later in a different hand:

The door no one uses…

The middle of the message had been smeared beyond recognition, as though someone had dragged a wet thumb across the ink. Only the end remained legible and the last words were:

… the worst thing about the holiday.

She knew the question. It was the answer that bothered her. Who was the dead man? That part was easy. The real problem was why he appeared in photographs spanning forty years, which door, and what holiday. She sorted through the rest of the papers. The copy of the will, the probate papers, all of it, those she remembered. She looked to see if she had missed anything else in the box.

The only thing left was an old tintype photo. The man in the photo looked like the dead man standing in front of a painting she had seen before but couldn’t quite place. She flipped it over and there was a note attached to the back. She carefully removed it as it was brittle and cracking as she unfolded it and it read:

My Dearest,

How I miss you and long for the days of merriment we once shared. Your last letter left me both haunted and heartsick, particularly your words:

“The heaviness of empty arms is all I feel now.”

I have carried that sorrow with me ever since. There are moments when I fancy I hear your footsteps in the hall or catch your reflection where none should be. Yet each day ends the same way, with the distance between us no smaller than before.

I hope this small token may ease both their emptiness and the burden upon your heart.

Until we meet again, I remain,

Yours always,

Q.

Who is Q? she thought to herself. No relative she knew of had a name that began with the letter Q. She kept pondering on Q and the man when a thought struck her. She grabbed the tintype and the crime scene photos to compare them, but then she noticed something in both of them besides the man. “Why can I never find the magnifying glass when I need it?” she said to no one in particular. She located it in a box in the kitchen labeled, ‘Junk drawer,’ and started studying the crime scene photos. Reflected in the mirror was the same painting the man in the tintype was standing in front of. Who is Q and where are the painting and bathroom? What does it have to do with my grandmother? Is this family?

Her mind raced through every piece of family history she knew, but there wasn’t a clear answer. Her thoughts were interrupted when she heard footsteps on the wooden floor outside in the hallway. Then the door slammed shut, but no one was there.

She leaned back in her chair and looked through the office doorway. For just a moment she thought she caught a glimpse of a man entering the bathroom at the end of the hall.

She stood.

The bathroom door was closed.

She didn’t remember closing the bathroom door.

She tip-toed down the hallway, listening for any movement or sound of someone in the bathroom. As she got closer to the bathroom door it began to change. She paused and looked again. Wait, that was supposed to be a door and now it’s a window. She took a few steps back toward the office.

The door is back.

A few steps forward.

Changed to a window.

Maybe it was not your ordinary door?

She started forward again but paused as something whispered, “Careful.” It was the advice she often heard but never listened to. She took a few more steps forward, but the seeds were sown. This was no ordinary door. Now it completely disappeared and the open door to the bathroom was back.

She laughed at herself as she caught her reflection in the mirrored shower doors. The house seemed to sigh along with her, as though relieved by the laughter. Almost as if it were saying, “And there you have it,” mystery solved with mirrors. She looked around the bathroom and realized just how bare it was. One of the few places her grandmother had not kept open. She spoke to the house again. “There is no time like the present to spruce you up.” She busied herself putting out towels, rugs, and cups, turning the bathroom into what her grandmother called the powder room.

She kept herself busy, putting away the last of her things, trying not to think about the tintype or the leather folder. She was mentally taking inventory of what things remained and what needed to be replaced when she found herself in the garden. A memory popped into her head when she was last here. Her grandmother had hosted a debutante ball for her sixteenth birthday. It wasn’t until after that she discovered sometimes the darkest things hide in the light.

She remembered that night fondly. She had never put down roots anywhere because they were always bouncing around the globe, following her father from naval base to naval base. During her sixteenth year, they stayed with her grandmother while he was stationed nearby. She worried about the fuss her grandmother was making and her grandmother told her, “It wasn’t the first time this old house had seen a party.” She began to regale her with the stories of parties and balls from years ago.

Then she remembered the photographs. She raced back to the study and started looking for the albums. She finally found them on a shelf almost hidden in the back, but the strangest thing was that the last book on the shelf was a Bible. Aha! She thought. If there was a Q in the family tree, she was certain it would be recorded in there.

She took it and the photo albums back to the desk to study. She opened the Bible to where the births, marriages, and deaths were recorded and scanned the pages for anyone with the name of Q. She thought perhaps the ink had bled on the letter and ‘O’ had signed it, but there was only one O, Olive, who had married a man named Thomas. She didn’t think Q was an Olive. Perhaps the most dangerous thing about certainty is the false belief that every question has an answer waiting to be found.

She closed the Bible with a thud, disappointed it hadn’t resolved the question of Q. She sighed and picked up the first album. It was the oldest, and she began to flip through the pages. She realized she wasn’t the greatest fan of the older albums. The paper the old photos were attached to was brittle, leaving black flakes on the desk, and the glue and tape holding the pictures in place had long since lost their stickiness. One thing she noticed was that pictures were missing. The darkened area surrounded by faded black indicated something was once there. Some she vaguely remembered, and others she wondered about.

She laid the album on the desk, hoping not to damage it any further. As she adjusted its position, she accidentally bumped the leather folder to the floor. The photos spilled out and as she reached over to pick them up, she wondered… Yes, she thought to herself, treat it like a jigsaw puzzle. The pictures had to be the missing ones from the album. Some things are not as easy as they seem. The pictures did not have dates and not all of them fit neatly into the context of the surrounding pictures.

After spending hours reconstructing the albums and replacing the tape to hold them in place, she started using the Bible as a guide, the clothing as a timeline, and tried to match people to the ones in the pictures. As she continued to build the family tree on her notepad, she noticed another pattern emerging. The painting. It first appeared in what had to be the late 1800s to the early 1900s. That matched with the tintype. She mumbled aloud, “Geeze, talk about what is held between the spaces. Answers are not one of them.”

Finishing the tree, she was no closer to solving who Q was even with things like dance cards and invitations taped among the pictures. She went back and began tracing the painting, trying to figure out where it was and why it looked so familiar. Then she discovered something. Another picture appeared in its place, taken at what appeared to be her grandmother’s sixteenth birthday ball. The one that was now in the area that shared duties as a ballroom and dining room. She found the truth inside, but it was not the one she expected.

She gathered up several of the photographs including the crime scene ones, the tintype, and the magnifying glass, crossing the house to the dining room. She laid everything out on the long table and turned to the painting. She stood back and looked at the painting, studying it closely. What struck her first was what had always attracted her to the painting. The red dress.

She remembered her mother telling her grandmother, “She has never seen anything like it. She very much wants it as her ballgown.”

“I see no good reason to deny her the request. We are about the same size and it’s in a plastic bag in a trunk downstairs. I spent way too much time in that dress posing for the painting and standing in too tight shoes. I figured they would stretch as I wore them, but I learned my lesson and I never did that again. Let that be a lesson to you, young lady when you choose your shoes.”

She chuckled at that memory, and it was one piece of advice she had taken to heart. She was about to turn away and go downstairs when the reflection of the painting caught her eye in one of the mirrors. It was only part of the painting, but it was enough for her to realize that the painting had been there all along. Only now, her grandmother had been painted on top of it.

All the photographs removed, the painting altered, the crime scene photos, it felt like she was wandering around in a fog of ghosts. She snorted. She didn’t know if it was the universe, the house, or ghosts, playing with her but something definitely gave her a warning. She remembered thinking this morning on a walk, the cold, misty fog was a portent, but brushed it off. She had thought that dawn stole all the colors this morning until she reentered the house.

That solved the painting mystery, but not Q or why the dead man in the tub looked like him, nor what did all of this have to do with her family. She turned all she had learned over in her mind with what she knew, not realizing she had wandered out into the front hall. She looked up and with a start realized she was standing at the bottom of the grand staircase next to the old grandfather clock.

This triggered another memory a few days after her ball. She awoke suddenly and realized the house was silent. Not empty, just silence, save for a hollow and echoing, slow, dripping sound coming from the downspout next to her window. She listened a moment longer and began to hear the hollow footsteps of movement across the wooden floors. She relaxed and went back to sleep.

Later that evening, the town’s clocks all stopped at the same moment except the one in her grandmother’s hallway, and when it struck thirteen, no one else seemed to notice. It was an eerie sound reminiscent of a broken harp string and a flat bell rolled into one. She crept down the staircase and paused looking at the clock, wondering why she was the only one there.

She spotted an open door reflected in the glass pane of the clock and turned around to look. She was careful not to make a sound in her sock feet and crept closer to the door. She had never noticed it before. She wondered where the door went. It was open just enough she could squeeze through and found herself standing on a landing at the top of a spiral staircase. She leaned over and looked down when the angry voice of her grandmother drifted up.

“That was taking care of it?”

Her grandfather and great-grandfather said together, “Yes!”

Her grandmother slammed something onto the table. “I thought we were rid of the Quanders once and for all when we got rid of the painting. I didn’t expect murder!”

Her great-grandfather replied, “How was I supposed to know he had an illegitimate child.”

“The whole family has been illegitimate from the beginning. There was never any proof Quincy was the product of my great-grandmother and a tryst with Quincy’s father. We all learned that story when he started showing up with that letter and the tintype. She was dead and couldn’t provide proof either way and nothing in her diaries indicated anything of the sort. They met, fell in love, and he sent her the painting instead of coming back to her. The one you stuck me into!”

“Sweetheart you need to calm down.”

“Don’t tell me to calm down.”

“Then at least lower your voice before you wake the house and they find out.”

Too late, she thought. She used to think the worst thing that could happen was her father being killed in the line of duty. Now three of her brightest lights were clouded in darkness. Murder?

Her grandmother kicked a box, sending it scraping across the floor. “They are gonna find out sooner or later because the detectives keep coming around. They don’t believe his drowning in the tub was an accident or why he was in our private bathroom!”

Her great-grandfather replied, “That was how we kept him occupied so he wouldn’t ruin her party. They know he showed up uninvited, tried to crash the party, we put him in the bedroom and told him to clean himself up because he was drunk and then when you went to check on him, he had drowned in the tub.”

“And the belladonna we slipped into his bicarbonate?”

“Honey you know we only did that to calm him down and keep him quiet. All they found was the alcohol and it’s going to be ruled accidental drowning due to intoxication. The belladonna doesn’t matter. Since you only keep it to help you sleep and locked in the cabinet, they aren’t going to find it and haven’t yet. Even so, what they don’t know won’t hurt us, unless you want to tell them.”

“I don’t. I just want it to end. Four generations is enough.”

She jumped because the pipes began rattling as water rushed through them. She squeezed back through the door and bolted back to her room before she was caught eavesdropping. She pretended to just wake up when her door opened and the light from the hallway hit her face.

“Sorry. Didn’t mean to wake you. Just checking if you were awake because I heard the water running in my room.”

“No, it’s okay Grandma. I was just going back to sleep. I guess that is what I heard and not the other.”

Her grandmother came and sat next to her on the bed. “The other?”

“Yes. I thought the clock struck thirteen times and then the radio said all of the clocks in town were out because of a power outage.”

“Oh. Yeah, that. Sometimes we miscount and if you heard the old grandfather make a flat sound, well that was just it welcoming you home. It only does that for people who belong here and you will always belong here.”

Her grandmother kissed her forehead and tucked her in tight. “Now go back to sleep and don’t worry about the sounds of the house talking to you.”

The sounds of the house talking to me. She pondered for a moment and then added, and the photographs, the painting, and a bathroom. She thought about all she had learned and now years later, what she heard that night made sense. Did it change how she felt? No. Even back then it might have bothered her, but at the end of the day she really was home. It was the first time she’d had it.
